SIGReg, Interactively

SIGReg is an anti-collapse regularizer built from three tools: characteristic functions, the Epps–Pulley test, and the Cramér–Wold theorem. This companion lets you do more than read about it — drag the sliders, run gradient descent live, and watch each piece work.

§1 · CHARACTERISTIC FUNCTIONS
Seeing a distribution’s fingerprint
The characteristic function is φX(t) = E[eitX]. Each sample zn maps to a point eit·z_n on the unit circle; averaging those points gives the empirical characteristic function φN(t). Drag t and watch a different fingerprint appear for each distribution.

At small t, the angles cluster near 0 and the centroid sits close to 1. At large t, the angles wrap around and scatter, so the centroid drifts toward the origin. Discrete distributions (coins) keep oscillating no matter how large t gets, while the Gaussian’s fingerprint decays smoothly to 0 — which is exactly why §3 needs a weight function.
§2–3 · EPPS–PULLEY & BANDWIDTH
Turning “how far from Gaussian” into one number
The Epps–Pulley statistic integrates the gap between the empirical CF and the target CF e-t²/2, weighted by the Gaussian window w(t) = e-t²/2λ². A small bandwidth λ only sees low moments (mean, variance); a large one sees the distribution’s full shape. Run gradient descent on a bimodal sample and see how λ changes the outcome.

With λ small (e.g. 0.3), the bimodal sample already matches the Gaussian’s mean and variance, so the test has nothing to fix and barely moves it. Around λ = 1, the test catches the higher-order shape mismatch, and the valley between the two modes fills in until the histogram looks unimodal and Gaussian — a live reproduction of Figure 3.5 in the article.
§5 · CRAMÉR–WOLD
One direction alone can be misleading
An anisotropic 2D Gaussian with σx=2, σy=0.5 gets projected onto a unit vector u. The projected variance — and the Epps–Pulley statistic T(u) — changes with direction. The Cramér–Wold theorem guarantees that if every direction’s projection matches N(0,1), the underlying distribution matches N(0,I) — which is why we need to average over many directions.

Dragging θ makes T(u) swing up and down. Certain directions (near the diagonal) let the over-wide x-axis and the too-narrow y-axis partly cancel out, so the projection looks “Gaussian enough” even though the joint distribution clearly isn’t. Add directions on the right and the running average converges to the true value at a rate of 1/√M.

§8 · THE BIG PICTURE
Putting it all together
SIGReg alternates exact mathematical equivalences (Cramér–Wold, Fourier uniqueness) with tractable numerical approximations (Monte Carlo, trapezoidal quadrature).
Goal: test whether Z ~ N(0, I_D)via the Cramér–Wold theorem (exact)
→ test u·Z ~ N(0,1) for every direction uvia Monte Carlo, M random directions (error ~1/√M)
→ average the Epps–Pulley statistic over M directionsvia uniqueness of characteristic functions (exact)
→ per direction: weighted integral of |φ_N(t) − e^(−t²/2)|²via trapezoidal quadrature, K frequency knots (error ~1/K²)
→ SIGReg(Z) = weighted sum of the gapscost per evaluation is O(MKN) — linear in M, K, and N

In the population limit, this value can only be zero when Z is exactly the isotropic Gaussian N(0,I). Since that Gaussian’s covariance is full rank, collapse simply cannot exist at this loss’s minimum.
